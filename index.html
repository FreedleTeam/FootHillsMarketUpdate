The southern foothills: where to buy, and what the market is doing

A buyer's guide for Kelly and Terry, prepared by Jake FreedleSingle-family homes from $800,000 to $1,025,000. Based on MLS sales that closed April 9, 2026 to October 2, 2026, plus active and pending listings as of early October 2026.

Buying in the foothills is two decisions at once: the market and the lot. The first half of this guide is what the last six months of sales say about your price range. The second half is how to judge a specific property up here, where fire exposure, sun, slope, access, water and utilities can each make or break daily life and resale.

The data covers Evergreen, Conifer, Morrison, Indian Hills and the Genesee area, plus a handful of Littleton addresses in Ken Caryl Valley and Deer Creek Canyon. I kept every sale in your price range and grouped them by the town in the mailing address.

One honest note on those names. A mailing address isn't the same as a community. Most of the Morrison addresses here sit up along US-285 and Turkey Creek, not in the town of Morrison. The Golden addresses are Genesee and Lininger Mountain. Evergreen Meadows shows up under both Evergreen and Conifer addresses. And Ken Caryl Valley is a suburban HOA neighborhood, a different kind of purchase from the rest of this list. I left it in so you can compare.

What this means for you

This range is split into two markets. The homes that are priced right and don't have an obvious problem still go in about a week, often at or above asking. Everything else sits, and the numbers show it: the typical active listing has been up 53 days, and 38 of the 54 have already reduced. Be ready to move quickly on the good ones, and negotiate hard on the ones that have been sitting. Before you do, find out why they've been sitting. Up here, the reason is often the lot, not the house.

Timing matters too. Closings ran 14 to 19 a month from April through July, then 11 in August and 9 in September. My read: mountain markets slow down more than the metro in winter. Expect fewer new listings, but more patient sellers on what's left.

Days on market is MLS days. "Sale vs. original list" compares the final price to the first list price, so price reductions count against it. Months of inventory is active listings divided by the average number of closings per month over these 5.8 months. I've left price per square foot out of the headline numbers because land, views and acreage swing values up here. The median was $329 per finished sq ft, but the middle 80% of sales ranged from $268 to $418.

Evergreen is half of this market, and it's where the gap between winners and everyone else is widest. Homes there that took longer than 30 days averaged 89.9% of their original price.

Conifer is the softest right now. It has about 4.7 months of inventory, a median of 21.5 days on market for closed sales, and 10 of its 13 active listings have reduced. If Conifer works for you, that's real leverage.

The Morrison addresses sold fastest, with a median of 10 days and 57% closing at or above their original price. But supply has built up there since: 11 are active now and 9 of them have cut their price. Indian Hills, the Genesee area and Littleton had too few sales to read much into.

Lots to be careful with

In the city I'd tell you which streets to avoid. Up here, it's which lots. This is about resale as much as daily life. Even if a steep north-facing driveway doesn't scare you, it will scare a lot of the buyers you'll eventually be selling to, and that shows up in your price and how long it takes to sell.

Where the land works against you

  • Canyon bottoms. They lose direct winter sun early, hold cold air and ice, and melt slowly. After a wildfire upstream, they also face flooding and debris flows.
  • Steep driveways that face north. Snow and ice stay on them much longer, and they can be impassable without four-wheel drive and good tires for much of the winter.

Where fire and access work against you

  • Single-access roads and long dead ends. In a fast-moving fire, one way out is a real risk.
  • Lots surrounded by forest that nobody has thinned. A well-mitigated house next to an overgrown lot is still exposed.

The neighbor rule: homes within about 100 feet of each other share wildfire risk, because their ignition zones overlap. Look at the lots next door as carefully as the one you're buying.

Where I'd focus

These aren't neighborhoods. Up here, where a home sits on its land matters as much as which subdivision it's in. These are the things that protect your price when you sell, because they matter to the widest pool of future buyers.

South-facing, ideally the living area and the driveway

South-facing homes get winter sun, melt snow faster, stay drier and cost less to heat. Hilltops and south slopes also get the best light and views.

The tradeoff: fire runs fastest uphill and ridgetops take more wind, so mitigation and roof and vent quality matter even more on these lots.

More than one way out

Subdivisions with two or more roads out are safer in a fire, and the next buyer will notice that too. Check that a fire engine can get up the driveway and turn around.

Stay off long dead ends, gated driveways and tight switchbacks.

Well-mitigated streets, not just a well-mitigated house

Look for thinned trees, short grass and space between homes up and down the street. Ask the seller for mitigation records and any fire district assessment.

Stay away from a clean lot surrounded by overgrown ones.

Roads that get plowed

County-maintained roads are simpler. A private road means a road association or HOA, so find out who plows, how fast, and what it costs.

Drive the route in winter conditions if you can.

The 1 to 3 acre home that works in winter

This was the steadiest part of the market. A flat spot to park, a garage you can reach in snow, and a manageable driveway keep it that way.

Get a real number for a plow contract on any long driveway.

Homes that have been sitting, for a fixable reason

23 of the 54 active listings in your range have been up more than 60 days. Some are overpriced, and some need work you can do.

Skip the ones sitting because of the lot itself. You can't fix a canyon bottom.

Wildfire: the first thing to check

Know the risk and the rules for the address

Look up the property's hazard rating with the fire district that serves it. Most districts publish a Community Wildfire Protection Plan that rates small areas, and one neighborhood name can include very different ratings a short drive apart.

Jefferson County's Wildfire Resiliency Code took effect July 1, 2026 for unincorporated areas inside the county's wildland-urban interface overlay. It isn't a retrofit mandate. It applies to new construction and to exterior work like reroofing, re-siding, deck replacement and additions. If the roof, deck or siding is near the end of its life, assume the replacement will need to meet the new standard and price that in. Some Evergreen addresses are in Clear Creek County, and the Town of Morrison has its own rules, so confirm which applies.

For a neighborhood-by-neighborhood look at fire districts, hazard ratings and ways out, see my Foothills Wildfire Guide.

Mitigation

The Colorado State Forest Service names two things that most decide whether a home survives a wildfire: fire-resistant roofing, siding and decks, and a defensible zone around the house. Its Home Ignition Zone guide splits the area around a home into three zones.

On a slope, the zones should extend farther on the downhill side. When you look at a home, check the roof, vents, siding near the ground and the deck. Ask for mitigation records, and ask the fire district about a home ignition zone assessment. Many districts do these for free. Then price in whatever mitigation the home still needs.

Getting out

  • Prefer more than one road out of the subdivision.
  • Make sure a fire engine can get up the driveway and turn around.
  • Note the distance to the nearest fire station and whether it's staffed or volunteer.
  • Sign up for county emergency alerts the day you move in. In Jefferson County that's LookoutAlert.

Insurance

Get insurance quotes during your inspection period, before your deadlines pass. Carriers are declining more mountain properties, and an uninsurable house is one your lender won't close on.

The Colorado FAIR Plan is a last resort, not a plan. It requires declinations from three carriers, caps residential coverage at $750,000, and pays actual cash value (replacement cost minus depreciation) rather than full replacement cost. On a home that would cost more than that to rebuild, it can leave a real gap.

Mitigation can help with price. Since July 1, 2025, Colorado insurers that use wildfire risk models have to explain a property's score and what drove it, post the mitigation discounts they offer, and let you appeal a score if you have evidence of work done. Keep receipts and dated photos.

One more thing: insurers can stop binding new policies near an active wildfire. A fire nearby during your contract can delay a closing.

The rest of the land

Sun and orientation

Aim for at least part of the house, ideally the main living area and the driveway, facing south. North-facing lots keep snow and ice on driveways and decks much longer. Canyon bottoms lose sun early in winter and hold cold air.

Driveway and access

  • Walk the driveway and ask the seller how they handle it in snow.
  • Note whether the garage is at the top or bottom, and whether there's a flat spot to park and turn around. 5 of the 88 homes that sold in your range had no garage at all.
  • Confirm whether the road is county-maintained or private, and who plows.
  • Get a real number for driveway snow removal.

Water and septic

Find out whether the home is on public water, a well, or a cistern with hauled water. Each changes cost, reliability and resale.

  • Well permits set what the water can be used for, and some don't allow outdoor watering. Pull the permit from the Colorado Division of Water Resources and compare it with how you want to use the property.
  • Order a well flow test and a water quality test. A shared well needs a written agreement.
  • For septic, get the system's age, permit and pumping records, and order an inspection. Replacement is expensive, and slopes and soils can limit the options.

Utilities and connectivity

  • Propane is common. Find out whether the tank is owned or leased and what a year of fuel costs.
  • Mountain power goes out more often, and utilities may cut power during extreme fire weather. Ask about outage history and whether there's a generator.
  • Check the actual internet options at the address and test cell service on site. If you work from home, this can decide the purchase.

Everything else

  • Radon: elevated levels are common in Colorado. Test every home.
  • Wind, snow and hail: check the roof's condition and history, and make sure decks can handle snow load.
  • Wildlife: bears, mountain lions and elk are part of life. Bear-resistant trash storage may be required.
  • Slope and drainage: look for erosion, drainage toward the house, retaining walls and rockfall. Steep lots limit additions and add cost to any project.
  • Emergency care: note the drive to the nearest hospital and the ambulance service that covers the address.
